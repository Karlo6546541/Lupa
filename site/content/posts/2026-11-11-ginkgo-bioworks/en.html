Ginkgo Bioworks is a company many investors have written off. Five years ago it was the poster child of the synthetic-biology boom; today the market values it at about 0.8 billion dollars, a fraction of the losses it has accumulated. Its accumulated deficit stood at 6.32 billion dollars on 30 September 2026. That is exactly what makes it interesting: over the past two years the company has changed its business model and now sells something fairly concrete — robotic laboratories that run without constant human supervision.

What Ginkgo sells today

Management describes the business as two offerings on one platform. The first is autonomous labs as equipment: Ginkgo sells systems built from RACs (reconfigurable automation carts, mobile carts carrying lab instruments) run by its Catalyst software. Customers include pharma companies, national laboratories, universities and the US government.

The second is Cloud Lab, the lab as a service. In Boston, Ginkgo runs Nebula, which it calls the world's largest autonomous lab; according to the earnings call it has 105 RACs. Customers order experiments there: ADME assays (absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion), DRUG-seq transcriptomics and antibody developability data. A growing share of orders are datasets for training AI models.

Management says the systems perform the equivalent of 200 to 300 technician hours on an average day, peaking at about 500. The pricing pitch is blunt: Ginkgo offers a package of five Tier 1 ADME assays for 199 USD and says similar services cost about 1,000 USD at competitors such as WuXi. It also stresses that the work is done in the US, which matters to pharma companies reducing their reliance on Chinese contract research organisations (CROs).

Ties to large companies

For a small company, the partner list in the Q3 release is impressive — but the relationships differ in kind:

  • Novo: the first sale of an autonomous lab to a top-tier pharma company, for its new R&D site in Waltham, Massachusetts.
  • Takeda, Argenx, Lundbeck and AbbVie founded Ginkgo's Antibody Developability Consortium; GSK, Novartis and Biogen signed new work.
  • OpenAI and other frontier AI labs, according to the company, use Cloud Lab for data generation.
  • Government: Pacific Northwest National Laboratory is getting a 97-RAC system; ARPA-H awarded a subcontract worth up to 17.5 million USD to bring robotics into RNA-medicine manufacturing; Ginkgo takes part in the NSF's roughly 400 million USD Programmable Cloud Laboratories programme (Caltech, the University of Maryland and Northwestern commissioned Ginkgo-built labs). On 8 October the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy (OSTP) said Ginkgo is providing Nebula access under the 1.5 billion USD X-Labs initiative.

One relationship ended expensively. According to the call, Ginkgo paid 14 million USD earlier in 2026 as part of a restructuring of its Google Cloud contract agreed in late 2025. Also worth remembering: big customer names say nothing about contract size. Total third-quarter revenue was 27.7 million USD.

Costs are falling fast: R&D excluding stock-based compensation dropped 48%, from 51 to 27 million USD. Yet nine-month revenue is 37% lower than a year ago, and the sequential rise from 20 to 28 million USD is not yet a trend. Revenue beat the analyst consensus (21.6 million USD, per Investing.com), but from a low base.

One balance-sheet problem still weighs on results: real estate. Non-current lease liabilities are 397 million USD, and the carrying cost of excess space (net of sublease income) was 14 million USD in Q3 alone and 44 million over nine months. It is a legacy of the expansion years, and spending that has nothing to do with the labs Ginkgo sells today.

Cash and dilution

Cash and marketable securities total 285 million USD. The company burned 120 million in nine months and expects 125 to 150 million for the full year. If spending stayed at the guided pace, existing cash would last roughly two years (Lupa calculation, assuming an unchanged burn rate and no new funding). The company is also selling shares through its ATM programme: 23.8 million USD gross over nine months, about 7 million net in Q3. Weighted average shares in Q3 were 63.97 million, about 15% more than a year earlier.

At a share price of {{px:DNA}} USD, the market cap is about 2.8 times the cash and securities on the balance sheet. Roughly speaking, the market values the operating business (excluding lease obligations) at a little over half a billion dollars. According to stockanalysis.com's aggregation, the average analyst rating is "sell", with an average price target of 8 USD.

Risks

  • Proof of demand. The autonomous-lab model is only starting to prove itself. One sale to a big pharma company is not yet a market.
  • Government dependence. A large share of new work comes from agencies (ARPA-H, NSF, national labs). The company itself flags the risk of shifting budget priorities and the effects of federal government shutdowns.
  • Competition. Cloud Lab competes with large CROs; the equipment business competes with established lab-automation vendors and with customers who can build such systems themselves.
  • Real estate and dilution. Until the excess space is dealt with, part of the burn is structural, and the ATM programme means constant pressure from new shares.
  • Volatility. Over 52 weeks the stock traded between {{lo52:DNA}} and {{hi52:DNA}} USD. Small biotech stocks are sensitive to rates and sector sentiment: on 10 November XBI fell {{chg:XBI}}.

Lupa's take

Ginkgo is no longer a story about "programming cells for everyone". It is an equipment-and-services company trying to become infrastructure for automated drug research, with a customer list that much larger companies would envy. The question for the next few quarters is whether revenue can grow faster than cash is spent, without fresh dilution. We will watch for further lab sales to pharma, the number of Cloud Lab customers and progress on the excess-space problem.
